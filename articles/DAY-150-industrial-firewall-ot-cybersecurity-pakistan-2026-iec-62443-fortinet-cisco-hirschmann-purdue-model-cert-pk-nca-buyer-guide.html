1. Why Pakistani Factories Can No Longer Ignore OT Cybersecurity

For years, Pakistani factories operated on the assumption that their industrial networks were safe because they were "air-gapped" or simply not connected to the internet. That assumption collapsed when remote access became standard during COVID-19, when ERP systems were connected to production floors, and when export buyers began auditing supplier cybersecurity as a condition of continued business.

The Threat Landscape in Pakistan

CERT-PK (Pakistan's national Computer Emergency Response Team under PTA) issued advisories in 2023 and 2024 specifically warning industrial operators about ransomware campaigns, spear-phishing targeting factory management systems, and APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) groups probing SCADA infrastructure in the power and water sectors. WAPDA's distribution automation systems — the SCADA networks managing LESCO, GEPCO, IESCO load dispatch — have been subject to attempted intrusions reported through NTDC's operations centres.

For textile and manufacturing exporters, the threat is now also commercial: EU buyers operating under the NIS2 Directive (October 2024) require supply chain cybersecurity assurance. CTPAT-certified importers in the US must verify that their Pakistani suppliers protect shipping and production data. A factory without documented OT/IT segmentation risks losing its export approval.

The Regulatory Push

Pakistan's National Cyber Security Authority (NCA) published its Critical Information Infrastructure Protection (CIIP) directive in 2024, designating power, water, gas, telecom, and large-scale manufacturing as critical sectors. PSQCA is developing cyber requirements for industrial equipment certifications. NEPRA's Grid Code now references cybersecurity obligations for DISCOs and IPPs operating SCADA. The window for voluntary compliance is closing; audits have begun.

The critical failure mode: When an IT firewall encounters an unknown Modbus TCP packet, it has two choices — pass it (allowing potentially malicious commands) or block it (causing a PLC to miss a command and potentially trip a machine). Neither outcome is acceptable. An industrial firewall with Modbus DPI can inspect the function code, register address, and value inside every Modbus packet, allowing legitimate reads while blocking write commands from unauthorised sources.

3. The Purdue Model & IEC 62443 Zone & Conduit Architecture Explained

IEC 62443 (the successor to ISA-99) is the international standard for Industrial Automation and Control System (IACS) security. Its Zone & Conduit model is the blueprint for segmenting a factory network. Combined with the Purdue Model (ISA-95), it gives Pakistani factory engineers a practical framework for network architecture.

Purdue Model Levels

  • Level 0 — Physical Process: Sensors, actuators, field instruments, motors, valves. No IP connectivity — 4–20mA, HART, Foundation Fieldbus, or digital I/O.
  • Level 1 — Basic Control: PLCs, RTUs, DCS controllers, safety systems (SIS). Direct connection to Level 0 field devices. Modbus RTU/RS-485, PROFIBUS, DeviceNet.
  • Level 2 — Supervisory Control: HMI stations, SCADA servers, engineering workstations, historian clients. Industrial Ethernet (PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, Modbus TCP).
  • Level 3 — Site Operations: MES, batch management, site historian server, quality management systems. OPC-UA to Level 2.
  • Level 3.5 — Industrial DMZ: The critical boundary. Data relay services, OPC-UA proxies, remote access jump servers, patch management servers. This is where the OT firewall lives.
  • Level 4 — Business Network: ERP, email, internet, cloud services. Standard IT infrastructure.

IEC 62443 Zones and Conduits

A Zone is a grouping of assets with a common security level requirement. A Conduit is a controlled communication path between zones — essentially a firewall rule set governing what can flow between them. IEC 62443 defines four Security Levels:

  • SL 1: Protection against casual or coincidental violation (most SME factories)
  • SL 2: Protection against intentional violation using simple means (recommended minimum for Pakistani factories with export buyers)
  • SL 3: Protection against intentional violation using sophisticated means (power utilities, pharmaceutical manufacturers)
  • SL 4: Protection against state-sponsored attacks (NTDC, WAPDA national grid)

For most Pakistani textile, food processing, and general manufacturing facilities, SL 2 is the target. This requires: network segmentation (zones), controlled data flows (conduits), authentication for all remote access, and documented incident response.

4. Dedicated Industrial Firewall: What Makes It Different

A dedicated industrial firewall is purpose-engineered for the OT environment. Here are the differentiating features that matter in Pakistan:

Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) for Industrial Protocols

Standard firewalls inspect packet headers. Industrial firewalls inspect the payload of industrial protocol packets:

  • Modbus TCP: Block unauthorised function codes (FC 05 Write Single Coil, FC 06 Write Single Register, FC 15/16 Write Multiple) while allowing FC 01–04 reads. This prevents an attacker who has breached the IT network from issuing write commands to PLCs.
  • DNP3: Inspect DNP3 Application Layer objects, block unauthorised direct operate commands, detect DNP3 replay attacks. Critical for WAPDA SCADA RTU protection.
  • EtherNet/IP (CIP): Inspect CIP service codes, block CIP Forward Open requests from unauthorised sources, detect CIP large forward open (CVE-2012-6435 class attacks).
  • IEC 61850 GOOSE: Pass GOOSE multicast traffic at wire speed (<1ms) while filtering unauthorised GOOSE publisher MACs. Essential for substation protection schemes.
  • PROFINET: Inspect RT/IRT frames, block AR (Application Relationship) establishment from unauthorised engineering stations.

Hardware for the Pakistani Factory Environment

  • Temperature: -40°C to +70°C operating range for extreme summer substation environments (Multan, Sukkur).
  • DIN rail mounting: Fits inside existing control panels without a separate rack cabinet — critical for space-constrained Pakistani factory switchrooms.
  • Dual 24VDC PSU: Primary and backup power inputs; works directly from UPS battery bus during WAPDA outages without AC/DC conversion losses.
  • Surge protection: IEC 61000-4-5 Level 3 (2kV line-to-line, 4kV line-to-ground) for WAPDA's notoriously high transient environment.
  • Conformal coating: Protection against humidity and airborne contaminants (textile lint, cement dust, sugar mill vapours).
  • MTBF >100,000 hours: No moving parts (fanless), industrial-grade components rated for 10+ year lifecycle.

6. OT Network Segmentation Step-by-Step: From Flat to Zone & Conduit

Most Pakistani factories currently operate a "flat" network — all devices (PLCs, HMIs, office PCs, printers, IP cameras) on the same VLAN, often sharing a consumer-grade router's subnet. This 5-step process moves to IEC 62443 Zone & Conduit without halting production.

  1. Step 1 — Asset Discovery and Zone Mapping (Week 1): Use a passive network scanner (deployed on a port-mirrored switch port — no active probing that could disrupt PLCs) to build a complete IP/MAC asset inventory. Group assets into draft zones: Zone A (Level 1–2 OT: PLCs, HMIs, SCADA), Zone B (Level 3: MES, historian), Zone C (Level 4: ERP, office), Zone D (DMZ: relay services).
  2. Step 2 — VLAN Design and Switch Reconfiguration (Week 2): On the managed industrial Ethernet switch, create VLANs for each zone. Configure inter-VLAN routing through the new OT firewall (not the layer-3 switch) so all cross-zone traffic is inspected. Test on a single non-critical segment first.
  3. Step 3 — OT Firewall Deployment at Level 3.5 DMZ (Week 3): Install the industrial firewall between the OT VLAN and the enterprise network. Begin with a permissive "log only" policy to capture all traffic flows. Analyse the firewall logs for 5–7 days to understand legitimate communication patterns before creating block rules.
  4. Step 4 — Conduit Rule Hardening (Week 4): Based on traffic analysis, create explicit allow rules for all legitimate OT-to-IT flows (historian uploads, SCADA alarm emails, remote access via jump server). Set default-deny. Test each PLC/SCADA function to confirm no legitimate traffic is blocked. This week is the highest-risk phase — schedule during a planned shutdown if possible.
  5. Step 5 — Remote Access Hardening and Documentation (Week 5): Deploy a dedicated industrial jump server (or secure remote access gateway) in the DMZ. Remove all direct VPN connections to OT devices. Document the zone map, conduit rules, and incident response procedure. Submit documentation to CERT-PK and to export buyer compliance portals.

7. CERT-PK Registration for Industrial Operators

CERT-PK (Computer Emergency Response Team Pakistan) operates under PTA (Pakistan Telecommunication Authority) and serves as Pakistan's national cybersecurity incident coordination body. Industrial operators — particularly those in power, water, manufacturing, and logistics — have specific obligations.

What CERT-PK Registration Covers

  • Registration on the CERT-PK portal as an "Industrial/Critical Infrastructure Operator" (separate category from general business registration)
  • Receipt of sector-specific threat intelligence advisories (WAPDA SCADA threats, ICS ransomware campaigns, industrial protocol vulnerabilities)
  • Access to CERT-PK's incident response assistance hotline (24/7 for critical infrastructure)
  • Mandatory inclusion in the national Cyber Incident Reporting System (CIRS)

Mandatory Reporting Thresholds

Under PECA 2016 and NCA CIIP 2024, industrial operators must report to CERT-PK within:

  • 1 hour: Active cyberattack causing production stoppage or safety system activation
  • 4 hours: Confirmed ransomware infection on OT/SCADA systems
  • 24 hours: Unauthorised access to any OT network component, data exfiltration, or service disruption >4 hours
  • 72 hours: Discovered breach (after forensic confirmation) even if no immediate impact was observed

Incident Response Obligations

Registered operators must maintain a documented Incident Response Plan (IRP) covering: detection (SIEM/log review), containment (firewall isolation procedures), eradication (malware removal from OT environment), recovery (control system restoration sequence), and post-incident review (root cause analysis submitted to CERT-PK within 30 days of major incidents).

8. NCA Cybersecurity Framework for Critical Infrastructure Pakistan

The National Cyber Security Authority (NCA) Pakistan published its national cybersecurity framework and CIIP directive in 2024. Industrial operators need to understand which obligations apply to them.

Sectors Designated as Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)

  • Energy: WAPDA, NTDC, DISCOs (LESCO, GEPCO, etc.), SNGPL, SSGCL, PSO, PPL — all SCADA/ICCP systems
  • Water: WASA systems in Lahore, Karachi, Faisalabad — SCADA/LIMS
  • Telecom: PTCL, Jazz, Telenor, Zong — network management systems
  • Finance: SBP, major commercial banks — payment infrastructure
  • Healthcare: PIMS, Aga Khan, major hospital chains — health IT and medical device networks
  • Large-scale Manufacturing: Factories with >500 employees or annual turnover >PKR 500 million whose production disruption would have national economic impact

NCA Framework Obligations for Industrial Operators

  • Annual cybersecurity audit by NCA-approved assessor
  • Documented cybersecurity policy and governance structure
  • Network segmentation between OT and IT (IEC 62443 alignment expected)
  • Vulnerability management programme for ICS assets
  • Employee cybersecurity awareness training (annual, documented)
  • CERT-PK registration and incident reporting compliance

PSQCA Interface

PSQCA (Pakistan Standards and Quality Control Authority) is developing cybersecurity requirements within its industrial equipment certification framework. While not yet mandatory for most factory equipment, PSQCA PS:4756 (industrial electrical safety) is expected to reference cybersecurity requirements for networked industrial devices in its next revision cycle. Factory managers sourcing new SCADA, PLC, or industrial communication equipment should request IEC 62443-4-2 component certification from suppliers to future-proof their procurement.

9. Textile & Manufacturing Export Buyer Cybersecurity Questionnaire

Pakistani textile, garment, and food processing exporters are increasingly receiving cybersecurity questionnaires from EU, UK, and US buyers as part of supplier qualification audits. Here is what each major framework requires.

C-TPAT (US Customs-Trade Partnership Against Terrorism)

C-TPAT Minimum Security Criteria (2020 revision) for Manufacturers includes cybersecurity requirements under Section 12. Pakistani factories certified or seeking certification as C-TPAT Tier 1/2 suppliers must demonstrate:

  • Written cybersecurity policy covering access control, password management, and network security
  • IT/OT network access controls preventing unauthorised changes to production or logistics systems
  • Documented cybersecurity incident response procedure
  • Employee cybersecurity awareness training (annual)
  • Protection of container/cargo tracking data and ERP shipping modules
  • Third-party vendor access controls (IT service providers, ERP vendors)

BCI Better Cotton Initiative & SMETA/Sedex

BCI's farm and gin certification requirements do not currently include OT cybersecurity mandates. However, SMETA (Sedex Members Ethical Trade Audit) 4-Pillar audits increasingly include data integrity questions under the Business Ethics pillar. Auditors are asking about:

  • Data security for worker records, payroll, and production data
  • Unauthorised access to production management systems
  • Chain of custody data integrity (cotton gin weighbridge data, yield records)

EU NIS2 Directive — Supply Chain Impact on Pakistani Exporters

The EU Network and Information Security Directive 2 (NIS2, effective October 2024) classifies textile, food processing, and manufacturing importers in EU member states as "important entities" with supply chain security obligations. While NIS2 does not directly bind Pakistani factories, EU buyers must demonstrate to regulators that their suppliers do not constitute a cybersecurity risk to their operations. The practical impact for Pakistani exporters:

  • EU buyers (German, Dutch, French, Belgian textile importers) are adding cybersecurity annexes to supplier contracts requiring basic OT/IT segmentation evidence
  • Factories without network segmentation documentation may fail EU buyer qualification audits from Q1 2025 onwards
  • Evidence required: network diagram showing OT/IT separation, firewall policy summary, CERT-PK registration certificate (if CII-designated), and incident response plan summary

11. 10-Point OT Cybersecurity Checklist for Pakistani Factory Managers

  1. Conduct an OT asset inventory. You cannot protect what you don't know exists. Use passive scanning (no active probing of PLCs) to map every IP device on your production network.
  2. Eliminate flat network architecture. If your office PCs and PLCs are on the same VLAN, your factory has zero OT cybersecurity. VLAN separation is the minimum first step.
  3. Deploy an industrial firewall at the OT/IT boundary. Place it at the Level 3.5 DMZ. Configure it with Modbus DPI at minimum. Enable logging immediately.
  4. Harden remote access. Replace all direct VPN access to OT devices with a jump server in the DMZ. Require MFA on all remote access. Review and revoke vendor access tokens after each maintenance visit.
  5. Patch engineering workstations. SCADA engineering PCs running Windows 7/10 without patches are the most common initial attack vector. These can be patched without touching PLCs.
  6. Remove default passwords. PLCs, HMIs, switches, and SCADA servers often ship with default credentials (admin/admin, 1234, etc.). Change every default password before connecting any device to the network.
  7. Register with CERT-PK. Even if not legally mandatory for your factory size, CERT-PK registration provides free threat intelligence and incident response support.
  8. Write an Incident Response Plan. A one-page plan covering: who to call, how to isolate the OT network (physically disconnect the firewall if necessary), and how to restore from known-good PLC program backups. Test it annually.
  9. Back up PLC programs and SCADA configurations. Store backups on an air-gapped medium (USB drive in a physical safe). Many ransomware attacks have succeeded because factories had no PLC program backup and had to pay to recover control logic.
  10. Train your team. Phishing and social engineering are the most common initial access vectors. Annual cybersecurity awareness training for anyone who touches a factory PC or mobile phone on the production network costs under ₨5,000 per employee online.

12. Frequently Asked Questions — OT Cybersecurity Pakistan 2026

What is the difference between IEC 62443 and IEC 62541 for Pakistani factories?

IEC 62443 is the cybersecurity standard for Industrial Automation and Control Systems (IACS) — it defines Zone & Conduit architecture, Security Levels (SL 1–4), and requirements for asset owners, integrators, and product suppliers. IEC 62541 is the OPC Unified Architecture (OPC-UA) communication protocol standard. In a Pakistani factory context, IEC 62443 tells you HOW to segment and protect your network; IEC 62541 defines the protocol used to securely communicate between devices across those segments. Both standards work together: OPC-UA implements built-in authentication and encryption that complements the IEC 62443 zone boundaries.

Can I use a standard UTM or IT firewall to protect my factory OT network?

No. Standard IT UTM firewalls fail in OT environments because they cannot perform Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) on industrial protocols such as Modbus TCP, DNP3, EtherNet/IP, or PROFINET. Their default security-over-availability posture can drop critical PLC commands. Their operating temperature range (0–40°C) is insufficient for Pakistani factory environments. A dedicated industrial firewall provides fail-open/fail-secure modes, industrial protocol DPI, WAPDA surge protection, and IEC 61850 GOOSE support.

Is CERT-PK registration mandatory for Pakistani industrial operators?

Under PECA 2016 and the NCA 2024 CIIP directive, critical infrastructure operators must register with CERT-PK and report incidents within 1–72 hours depending on severity. SME factories not classified as CII are encouraged but not legally mandated to register. However, EU/UK/US export buyer audit frameworks (CTPAT, BCI, NIS2 supply chain) increasingly require demonstrated incident response capability that effectively requires CERT-PK registration as evidence.

How often does NCA Pakistan audit critical infrastructure for cybersecurity compliance?

NCA conducts mandatory annual cybersecurity audits of designated CII operators. Power sector entities regulated by NEPRA, water utilities under WASA, and telecom operators under PTA face the most stringent schedules. Manufacturing facilities designated as CII face audit cycles every 12–18 months. All CII operators must maintain a documented Incident Response Plan and conduct tabletop exercises annually.

What are the five levels of the Purdue Model and which level needs the OT firewall?

Level 0 — Physical process; Level 1 — Basic control (PLCs/RTUs); Level 2 — Supervisory control (HMI/SCADA); Level 3 — Site operations (MES/historian); Level 3.5 — Industrial DMZ; Level 4 — Business network (ERP/internet). The OT firewall is deployed at Level 3.5 (DMZ boundary). A second firewall at Level 2/3 provides defence-in-depth for high-security applications such as power substations.

What cybersecurity requirements do CTPAT and BCI impose on Pakistani textile exporters?

C-TPAT Minimum Security Criteria requires Pakistani factory suppliers to implement IT/OT network access controls, documented cybersecurity policy, employee training, incident response capability, and protection of shipping data systems. BCI/SMETA audits increasingly include data integrity questions. EU NIS2 (October 2024) requires EU importers to verify supply chain cybersecurity, with Pakistani export factories needing to provide network segmentation evidence and incident response documentation to maintain EU buyer approval.

How do I design an Industrial DMZ for a Pakistani factory with limited IT budget?

A practical Industrial DMZ uses a three-firewall topology for mid-size factories (₨420,000–₨750,000 total): (1) OT firewall at Level 2/3 boundary; (2) DMZ zone hosting OPC-UA proxies and remote access jump servers; (3) IT firewall at Level 3.5/4 boundary. All OT-to-IT data flows through DMZ relay services — no direct connections. Start with a single OT firewall at Level 3.5 if budget is constrained; add the second firewall in Phase 2.

Have Pakistani factories been targeted by industrial cyberattacks?

Yes. Pakistan's power sector has experienced multiple SCADA-related incidents including APT41-attributed intrusions reported through NTDC operations centres. Pakistani textile factories have been hit by ransomware campaigns encrypting ERP and MES systems, with ransom demands from USD 20,000 to USD 150,000. The flat IT/OT network architecture common in Pakistani factories allowed ransomware to propagate directly into control systems, halting production for 2–8 days. CERT-PK's 2024 advisory specifically warned textile and pharmaceutical manufacturers about spear-phishing targeting factory management systems.
