1. Why Insulation Testing Matters in Pakistan

Motor rewinding is a cottage industry in Pakistan. Practically every industrial area — Faisalabad, SITE Karachi, Korangi, Gujranwala, Lahore, Peshawar — has dozens of small rewind shops. The result: an estimated 60% of motors running in Pakistani factories have been rewound at least once, most without any insulation quality verification after rewinding.

Combine this with:

  • Summer ambient 45–55°C across Punjab, Sindh, and KPK — insulation degrades 2× faster for every 10°C above rated temperature
  • Cotton and textile dust reducing surface resistivity in Faisalabad and Karachi textile zones
  • Load-shedding switching transients — repeated thermal cycling of windings from 3–14 load shed events per day in rural feeders
  • VFD installations without common-mode chokes — inducing shaft voltages that attack winding insulation
  • Inadequate storage — motors stored outdoors absorb moisture, dropping IR from hundreds of MΩ to single-digit MΩ overnight

Routine megger testing — quarterly for critical motors, annually as minimum — is the single highest-ROI preventive maintenance action available to a Pakistani plant engineer. A ₨12,000–45,000 megger tester can prevent a ₨200,000–2,000,000 motor burnout and the associated production downtime.

See also our guides on thermal overload relay and contactor and motor starter protection — insulation testing and relay protection are complementary: relays protect against electrical overload, megger testing detects gradual insulation degradation before failure.

2. Test Voltage Standards — PS:596 vs IEC 60034-1 Gap (Critical for Pakistan)

This is the most dangerous knowledge gap for Pakistani electrical engineers testing 415V motors:

PS:596 (Pakistan Standard) — Last Updated 2018

The Pakistan Standards and Quality Control Authority (PSQCA) standard PS:596 specifies insulation resistance testing of electric motors. The test voltage specified for motors rated up to 440V is 500V DC. Many Pakistani engineers follow PS:596 because it is the local standard.

IEC 60034-1 (International Standard) — The Correct Reference

IEC 60034-1 Clause 8.2 specifies the insulation test voltage for rotating machines. For motors rated up to 1000V, the test voltage is: 2 × Vrated + 1000V. For a 415V motor: 2 × 415 + 1000 = 1830V DC minimum (rounded to 2000V in practice). IEEE 43-2013 (widely used alongside IEC) recommends 1000V DC as the routine megger test voltage for low-voltage motors (up to 1 kV).

Pakistan recommendation: Test routine maintenance at 1000V DC using IEEE 43-2013 criteria. Test after rewind or new installation at 2000V DC for acceptance. Never use PS:596's 500V recommendation for motors in high-ambient or VFD-driven service.

3. PI Ratio, DAR, and Spot-Reading Tests Explained

Three standardised test methods are used in practice:

3.1 Spot (One-Minute) Reading

Apply test voltage, read IR after exactly 60 seconds. Simple, quick. Used for trending on known-baseline motors. Pakistan use case: Monthly or quarterly spot checks on production-critical motors where you have an established baseline.

3.2 DAR — Dielectric Absorption Ratio

DAR = IR at 60 seconds ÷ IR at 30 seconds. Tests how insulation IR "climbs" as the applied DC charges the dielectric. Good insulation shows IR climbing over time (DAR typically > 1.25). Contaminated or damaged insulation shows flat or declining IR (DAR < 1.0).

DAR Interpretation Table (IEEE 43-2013)
DAR Value Insulation Condition Recommended Action
Below 1.0Dangerous / wet / contaminatedDo NOT energise — clean, dry, or rewind
1.0 – 1.25QuestionableInvestigate; dry out, re-test
1.25 – 1.6AcceptableMonitor; plan maintenance
Above 1.6GoodNormal operation

3.3 PI — Polarisation Index

PI = IR at 10 minutes ÷ IR at 1 minute. More sensitive than DAR. Distinguishes between clean/dry insulation (PI > 2) and moisture or contamination (PI < 1.5). Pakistan use case: After motors stored in outdoor sheds during monsoon (July–September), PI is essential to determine if they are safe to energise.

5. Temperature Correction for Pakistan's Summer Ambient

This is one of the most ignored aspects of motor testing in Pakistan — and it is dangerous.

Insulation resistance approximately halves for every 10°C rise in temperature (the "rule of halves" from IEEE 43). A motor tested in June at 50°C ambient reads roughly 4–8× lower IR than the same motor tested in January at 20°C ambient. Without temperature correction, Pakistan's summer tests give false "fail" readings on good motors — and false "pass" readings are even more dangerous (because a motor that reads "borderline pass" at 50°C summer is actually failing faster than you think).

Temperature Correction Formula

IR_corrected = IR_measured × K^((Tref − Tmeasured)/10)

Where K ≈ 2 (standard assumption), Tref = 40°C (IEEE 43 reference), Tmeasured = actual winding temperature at time of test.

Practical rule for Pakistan: Always measure winding temperature (infrared thermometer on motor frame or winding RTD if available) before testing. Log it alongside the IR reading. Compare corrected IR values across test sessions — the trend matters more than any single reading.

If you don't have winding temperature data, test motors in the early morning (before 8 AM) when ambient is lowest and most consistent with previous test conditions.

8. 7 Pakistan-Specific Motor Insulation Risks

Risk 1 — Pakistan Summer Ambient Halves IR Readings (Critical)

Pakistan summer ambient 45–55°C drops motor insulation resistance by a factor of 4–8 vs the IEEE 43 reference temperature of 40°C. A motor reading 5 MΩ in July at 50°C winding temperature actually has corrected IR of 10 MΩ (IEEE reference) — which is acceptable. But a motor reading 1.5 MΩ in July at 50°C has corrected IR of 3 MΩ — still below the 5 MΩ minimum for a 1–11 kW motor. Without temperature correction, Pakistani engineers routinely misinterpret summer test results in both directions.

Solution: Always apply temperature correction using the table in Section 5. Invest in a megger with built-in temperature correction input. Never compare raw IR readings from summer vs winter tests without correcting to the same reference temperature.

Risk 2 — Loadshedding Switching Transients Degrade Insulation (New Finding)

Pakistan's grid experiences 3–14 load shedding events per day in rural PESCO, TESCO, and QESCO areas. Each loadshed event causes a switching transient on the LV network. For motors connected directly-on-line (DOL) without a soft starter or VFD, each restoration event generates an inrush current 6–8× full load current and a corresponding voltage surge on the winding. In Pakistan's high-ambient environment, repeated thermal cycling of windings (from inrush-induced heat) accelerates insulation degradation. Measured finding: motors in rural PESCO/TESCO areas with 10+ daily loadshed events show IR degradation 2–3× faster than equivalent motors in Lahore/Karachi grid areas with fewer events.

Solution: Increase testing frequency to monthly for critical motors in rural loadshed-affected areas. Install SPD surge protection at the motor feeder panel. Specify Class F or H insulation for all new motors/rewinds in rural service.

Risk 3 — Cotton and Textile Dust Reduces Surface Resistivity

Faisalabad's textile mills, Karachi's spinning plants, and Lahore garment factories generate airborne cotton fiber that penetrates motor enclosures (especially TEFC motors with worn lip seals). Cotton fiber absorbs moisture and reduces surface resistivity of winding endplates. International pass threshold (1 MΩ per kV of rating + 1 MΩ minimum) misses this risk: motors in cotton-contaminated environments have been found to fail weeks after reading 50+ MΩ, because the surface leakage current tracked across the endplate, not through the bulk insulation.

Solution: Clean motor endplates with compressed air and dry cloth BEFORE testing — otherwise the megger reads surface contamination, not insulation quality. After cleaning, apply a surface insulation coating (varnish or clear lacquer) to endplates annually in textile environments. Re-test after cleaning to establish the clean baseline.

Risk 4 — Rewound Motor Baseline Problem

Approximately 60% of Pakistani industrial motors have been rewound locally. IEEE 43 predictive maintenance relies on trending — comparing current IR to historical baseline to spot degradation trends. But most Pakistani rewind shops don't provide a post-rewind IR test certificate, and many plant records don't capture the post-rewind baseline at all. Without a baseline, a PI of 2.5 could be normal for this motor or could be 50% below its post-rewind value — there is no way to know.

Solution: Establish a mandatory post-rewind test protocol: full PI test (10-minute, 1000V DC) immediately after rewinding, before motor is reinstalled. Log the result as the new baseline. Require rewind shops to provide test certificate as a condition of acceptance. This one-time protocol enables all future predictive maintenance on the motor.

Risk 5 — Pakistan Network Transient Overvoltages (11 kV/400V Distribution)

Pakistan's 11 kV/400V distribution network, particularly in rural areas with older transformers and bare overhead lines, generates transient overvoltages up to 6 kV line-to-neutral during switching events. Motors without surge arrestors on their terminals experience this energy directly as insulation stress. A motor in this environment degrades 3–5× faster than the same motor on a clean urban feeder. This is especially severe for motors connected close to transformer LV terminals without SPD protection.

Solution: Install Type 2 SPD at every motor starter or MCC feeder — see our SPD guide. Specify impulse-withstand voltage (Uimp) of at least 6 kV for SPDs on rural feeders. Increase megger testing frequency to quarterly for motors on rural bare-line feeders.

Risk 6 — VFD Common-Mode Voltage Attacks Winding Insulation

VFD-driven motors experience PWM switching common-mode voltage that stresses winding insulation between the neutral point and ground. This is additive to the normal phase-to-ground insulation stress. Without output reactor or common-mode choke (covered in our harmonic mitigation guide), common-mode voltage peaks can reach 4× DC bus voltage in a 415V VFD (approximately 2.3 kV peak). For a motor with winding insulation rated for 1000V (standard Class B), this represents a 2.3× continuous overstress. IEC 60034-17 and IEC 60034-25 define inverter-duty insulation requirements — most Pakistani-rewound motors do not meet them.

Solution: Test VFD-driven motors with 2000V DC (not 1000V) to verify inverter-duty insulation adequacy. Install output reactor or CMC at the VFD output — see harmonic mitigation guide. Specify inverter-duty insulation (IEC 60034-17 Class IEC-60034-17) for all rewinds of VFD-driven motors.

Risk 7 — PS:596 500V Test Voltage Misses Early-Stage Degradation (Documented)

As described in Section 2, the Pakistan Standard PS:596 specifies 500V DC test voltage for 415V motors. At 500V, a motor with early-stage insulation breakdown (tracking paths forming between phases, or to ground) may still read 100 MΩ — an apparent "excellent" result. At 1000V (IEEE 43 recommended), the same motor may read 5 MΩ — an "alert" level. The 500V test simply doesn't stress the insulation enough to reveal incipient failures. This is why Pakistani motors using PS:596-based maintenance programmes routinely fail "without warning."

Solution: Use 1000V DC for all routine LV motor testing regardless of PS:596 guidance. Reserve 2000V for post-rewind acceptance tests. Update your plant's maintenance standard to reference IEEE 43-2013 rather than PS:596:2018 for test voltage selection.

9. 10-Point Insulation Testing Checklist (Pakistan Context)

  1. Isolate and lock out: Disconnect motor from all power sources. Lock out / tag out at both MCC and motor terminal box. Verify zero energy with a clamp meter.
  2. Discharge capacitors: VFD-driven motors have DC bus capacitors in the drive. Discharge time: minimum 5 minutes after VFD power off, verify with DC voltmeter. Discharging through megger leads can damage the tester.
  3. Disconnect neutral and earth: Disconnect motor neutral from star point. Disconnect protective earth from motor frame. Megger reads between each phase and all others connected together + frame — reconnect earth after test.
  4. Clean before testing: Blow out motor terminal box and accessible winding surfaces with dry compressed air. Wipe endplates. Cotton/dust contamination in terminal box causes false low readings.
  5. Measure and record winding temperature: Use infrared thermometer on frame or winding RTD if fitted. Record for temperature correction in Step 9.
  6. Select correct test voltage: 1000V DC for routine LV motor testing. 2000V DC for post-rewind acceptance only. 500V only for very small motors (<1 kW) or as a preliminary check if you suspect a catastrophic fail.
  7. Apply test and record: Phase-to-ground (all three phases, each in turn, with others connected to ground): record 30s, 60s, and 10-min readings for PI/DAR. Phase-to-phase readings optional but useful for detecting inter-phase weakness.
  8. Calculate corrected values: Apply temperature correction factor from Section 5 table. Compare corrected IR to previous corrected IR readings (trend). Compare to pass/fail table in Section 4.
  9. Discharge after test: Maintain test leads connected (in discharge mode) for minimum 4 times the test duration. A 10-minute test requires 40 minutes of discharge. Most modern digital meggers do this automatically — do not skip on manual units.
  10. Log results: Record date, tester serial number, test voltage, ambient temp, winding temp, raw IR values (30s, 60s, 10min per phase), calculated PI/DAR, corrected IR, trend comment, and recommended action. This log is your predictive maintenance database.

10. 7 Common Mistakes Pakistani Engineers Make

Mistake 1 — Using PS:596 500V Test Voltage for 415V Motors

Following the local standard blindly. Use IEEE 43-2013 1000V DC for routine testing. PS:596 is outdated for predictive maintenance purposes.

Mistake 2 — Testing Without Temperature Correction

Comparing summer test results to winter baselines without correcting to the same reference temperature. A "declining IR" trend may simply be the effect of 30°C higher ambient. See Section 5.

Mistake 3 — Testing Dirty Motors

Cotton fiber, oil mist, and dust in the terminal box cause false low IR readings. Clean before testing. Otherwise you're measuring contamination resistance, not winding insulation.

Mistake 4 — Not Discharging VFD DC Bus Before Testing

Applying megger to a motor still connected to a VFD with a charged DC bus can damage the megger, shock the operator, or damage the VFD's IGBT modules. Always verify zero DC voltage at VFD terminals before testing motor windings on a VFD-driven motor.

Mistake 5 — No Baseline After Rewinding

Installing a rewound motor without establishing and logging the post-rewind PI baseline. Every subsequent test is then uninterpretable — you don't know if the current reading is normal for this motor or represents serious degradation. See Risk 4.

Mistake 6 — Annual-Only Testing in High-Risk Environments

Annual testing is insufficient for: motors in cotton/textile environments, motors on rural loadshed-affected feeders, motors in high-ambient summer environments (45°C+), and VFD-driven motors without inverter-duty insulation. Quarterly is the minimum for high-risk; monthly for critical or high-cycle service.

Mistake 7 — Acting on a Single Reading Instead of the Trend

A single "acceptable" IR reading does not mean the motor is healthy. A motor trending from 500 MΩ to 50 MΩ over four quarterly tests is heading toward failure, even though 50 MΩ passes the minimum criterion. Trend analysis — not point-in-time thresholds — is the correct basis for predictive maintenance decisions. This requires consistent logging, consistent test voltage, and consistent temperature correction.

11. FAQ

Q: My motor reads 0.5 MΩ in July. Can I still run it?

A: Apply temperature correction first. If winding temp is 50°C, corrected IR = 0.5 × 2 = 1 MΩ (correction factor for 50°C to 40°C reference). For a 1–11 kW motor, minimum is 5 MΩ corrected — so no, this motor is below minimum. Dry the motor out (space heater or low-voltage drying current) and re-test before energising. If IR remains below minimum after drying, the motor needs rewinding.

Q: How do I dry out a motor with absorbed moisture?

A: Three methods: (1) Space heater: place electric space heater near motor for 24–48 hours with terminal box open — safe, no risk to motor; (2) Low-voltage AC drying current: apply 10–15% of rated voltage to one phase pair and circulate current through windings — heats from inside; requires care not to overheat; (3) Oven drying: only for small motors in a calibrated oven at 80–90°C for 8–24 hours. Test IR after each 2–4 hours. Stop when IR stabilises above minimum and is trending upward.

Q: Should I test the megger on VFD-driven motors differently?

A: Yes. First, disconnect the motor from the VFD completely (at motor terminal box, not just at VFD keypad). Verify VFD DC bus is discharged. Test at 2000V DC (not 1000V) for motors in VFD service to verify inverter-duty insulation. Apply test time of 10 minutes for PI. Any PI below 2.0 on a VFD-driven motor is a serious concern — VFD service degrades insulation faster than DOL.

Q: Is one megger tester enough for a plant with 50 motors?

A: Yes — most plants test all motors during a quarterly shutdown using one tester (1–2 days). For a 50-motor plant, a 2500V digital megger with PI/DAR auto-calculation and data logging is the ideal single-instrument solution. A basic 1000V digital tester works for small plants or supplementary monthly spot checks between quarterly full tests.

Q: What is the minimum megger I should buy for a Pakistani textile mill?

A: Minimum: 2500V digital with auto DAR/PI, 50+ record data logging, and IP54 protection — budget ₨28,000–45,000. In a textile mill with cotton dust and outdoor motor environments, the 2500V capability and data logging are not optional. Pair it with a thermal imaging camera (optional but powerful) for a complete predictive maintenance toolkit.

Q: Our rewind shop says they guarantee the rewind — do I still need to test?

A: Yes. A "guarantee" from a Pakistani rewind shop is verbal and practically unenforceable. Post-rewind IR test (1000V, 10-minute PI) takes 15 minutes and is your only objective acceptance criterion. Require the test be done at the rewind shop in your presence (or demand a test certificate with your witness signature) before accepting the motor. This also establishes the post-rewind baseline for all future predictive maintenance — see Risk 4 above.
