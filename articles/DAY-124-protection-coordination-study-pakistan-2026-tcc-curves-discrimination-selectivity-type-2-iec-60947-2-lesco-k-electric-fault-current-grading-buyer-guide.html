Protection Coordination Study Pakistan 2026 — TCC Curves, IEC 60947-2 Type 2 Selectivity, LESCO/K-Electric Fault Current Grading & Protective Relay Price Guide

Bottom line up front: A well-executed protection coordination study ensures that only the fuse, MCB, or relay closest to a fault operates — leaving the rest of the system energised. In Pakistan's LESCO and K-Electric networks, where fault levels at LT bus bars range from 10 kA to 50 kA, a missing or incorrect coordination study means nuisance tripping of feeders, transformer damage, and NEPRA compliance failures. This guide covers the full methodology: TCC curve construction, IEC 60947-2 Type 2 selectivity requirements, fault current grading for Pakistani distribution voltages, and a 2026 PKR price table for protective relays, trip units, and overcurrent relay test sets.

1. What Is Protection Coordination?

Protection coordination (also called protective device selectivity or discrimination) is the deliberate arrangement of protective devices in a power system so that the device nearest the fault operates first, and upstream devices remain closed. The objectives are:

  • Fault isolation: Disconnect the minimum amount of load.
  • Equipment protection: Prevent transformer, cable, and switchgear damage from thermal and mechanical stress.
  • Continuity of supply: Critical for hospitals, data centres, and process industries on LESCO/K-Electric tariff.
  • NEPRA compliance: Grid Code 2023 Section 6.3 requires fault clearance within defined time windows for utility-connected generation and 11 kV feeders.

Pakistan-specific context: LESCO 11 kV feeders typically carry 150–400 A load; prospective short-circuit current at LT panel incomers in industrial zones (Kot Lakhpat, SITE Karachi, Sundar Industrial Estate) can reach 35–50 kA(peak). Without coordination, a bolted fault at a sub-distribution board trips the 11 kV feeder breaker — blacking out hundreds of consumers.

2. Time-Current Characteristic (TCC) Curves — Construction & Interpretation

2.1 What Is a TCC Curve?

A TCC (Time-Current Characteristic) curve plots the operating time of a protective device against the fault current on log-log axes. The x-axis is current (A or multiples of In), the y-axis is time (seconds). Each device has a characteristic band defined by its minimum and maximum operating times.

2.3 Reading Discrimination Margins on TCC

For time-based discrimination, a minimum margin of 0.3–0.4 s is required between adjacent devices at the maximum fault current each device can see. This margin accounts for:

  • Relay overtravel time (electromechanical): 0.05 s
  • Circuit breaker opening time: 0.05–0.08 s (3–5 cycles at 50 Hz)
  • CT error and relay tolerance: 0.05–0.10 s
  • Safety margin: 0.10–0.15 s

For numerical relays (e.g., SEL-351, Alstom MiCOM P142), the total opening time of downstream device plus grading margin applies. Many Pakistan utility installations use Very Inverse (VI) or Extremely Inverse (EI) curves per IEC 60255-151 because these curves provide better discrimination for high-impedance faults common in overhead distribution networks.

3. IEC 60947-2 Selectivity — Type 1 vs Type 2

IEC 60947-2 Annex A defines two selectivity levels for LV circuit breakers working in combination (usually an upstream MCCB backing up a downstream MCB):

Selectivity TypeMeaningConsequence for Downstream MCCBPakistan Recommendation
Type 1 Upstream and downstream devices may both trip (cascade). Downstream device may sustain damage but must remain operational after fault clearance. Downstream MCCB may need inspection after major fault. No guarantee of discrimination at full fault level. Acceptable for non-critical branch circuits (general lighting, small motors). Requires post-fault inspection protocol.
Type 2 ✅ PREFERRED Only downstream device trips. Upstream device remains closed. Downstream device must not sustain damage — contacts may weld but device remains operational. Downstream MCCB must be tested or certified to remain closed at the declared Ics under the upstream device's let-through energy. Required for: hospitals, data centres, process industries, LESCO/K-Electric grid-connected premises, solar IPP LT panels (NEPRA NEM 3.0).

3.1 How to Achieve Type 2 Selectivity in Practice

  1. Consult manufacturer discrimination tables. Every reputable MCCB/MCB brand publishes selectivity tables specifying the maximum fault current at which Type 2 selectivity is achieved for each upstream–downstream pair. Do NOT assume selectivity based on rating alone.
  2. Match the let-through energy (I²t). The upstream device's let-through I²t at fault current must not exceed the downstream device's withstand I²t. Numerical evaluation: I²t(upstream let-through) ≤ I²t(downstream withstand).
  3. Use electronic trip units (ETU) with Zone Selective Interlocking (ZSI). For ACBs and large MCCBs, ZSI communicates instantaneous-zone signals between upstream and downstream devices, enabling the downstream device to trip instantaneously while the upstream device adds a brief delay — achieving full Type 2 discrimination even at maximum fault current without time delay penalty.
  4. Apply cascading (back-up protection) rules carefully. Cascading tables in IEC 60947-2 Annex A allow a lower-rated MCCB to operate behind a higher-rated upstream device. The upstream device limits the let-through current, protecting the downstream device even if it interrupts beyond its Icu. This is common in Pakistan for cost optimisation — e.g., a 160 A MCCB (Icu 36 kA) behind a 630 A MCCB (Icu 50 kA) in a Lahore industrial panel with 40 kA prospective fault current.

4. Fault Current Grading — Pakistan Distribution Network

4.1 Fault Level Calculation Chain

The fault current grading hierarchy from NTDC 132 kV bus bar down to LT final circuit:

  1. NTDC 132 kV bus: Typically 25–40 kA (system fault level varies by zone — Lahore Grid Station ~31.5 kA, SITE Karachi ~25 kA).
  2. 11 kV primary switchgear / RMU: Fault current limited by 132/11 kV transformer impedance (typically Zk = 10–12.5%). At a 5 MVA transformer: Isc(11kV) = 5000/(√3 × 11 × 0.12) ≈ 2.18 kA (3-phase); For 10 MVA, ≈ 4.37 kA.
  3. 11 kV cable / feeder: Further limited by cable impedance. Typical LESCO 11 kV XLPE underground feeder Zk ≈ 0.2–0.5 Ω/km.
  4. LV bus bar (415 V, after distribution transformer): Isc(415V) = 1000 kVA / (√3 × 415 × Zk). For 1000 kVA at 6%: Isc = 1000/(1.732 × 0.415 × 0.06) ≈ 23.2 kA. This is the value that must be compared to MCCB/fuse breaking capacity at the LT incomer.
  5. Sub-distribution board: Reduced by cable impedance between main and sub-panel. Must still be calculated — do not assume below 10 kA without calculation.

4.2 LESCO and K-Electric Grid Coordination Requirements

LESCO (Lahore Electric Supply Company):

  • 11 kV feeder OC relay setting: Phase OC pickup 100–200 A (11 kV base), TMS 0.1–0.3 (Very Inverse per IEC 60255), instantaneous element at 800–1200 A.
  • Earth fault relay pickup: 10–30 A (11 kV base), TMS 0.05–0.1.
  • Grading margin with downstream LT fuses: 0.3–0.4 s at maximum LT fault current referred to 11 kV.
  • LESCO typically uses AREVA / Alstom MiCOM P142 or P143 feeder protection relays. Replacement testing requires relay injection test set (Omicron CMC 256 or equivalent).

K-Electric (Karachi):

  • 33 kV and 11 kV distribution — K-Electric operates a combined 33/11 kV network in Karachi.
  • Feeder protection: Numerical relays (SEL-351S, Schneider Sepam 10) set to Standard Inverse (SI) for main feeders, Very Inverse (VI) for sub-transmission.
  • Large industrial consumers (connected at 11 kV) must submit protection coordination study to K-Electric Engineering for approval before energisation. Study must show TCC curves for all devices from 11 kV incomer down to LT panel level.
  • Required documentation: Single Line Diagram, Equipment data sheets, Fault level calculation, TCC curve plots (per IEC 60255), proposed relay settings file.

4.3 Step-by-Step Coordination Study Methodology

  1. Collect system data: Source impedance at PCC, transformer MVA and Zk, cable lengths and conductor sizes, all protective device ratings.
  2. Calculate fault levels: Three-phase fault (maximum), single-phase-to-earth fault (minimum) at every bus bar. Use IEC 60909 methodology.
  3. Select device ratings: Each device must have Icu (ultimate breaking capacity) ≥ prospective fault current at its location.
  4. Plot TCC curves: Starting from the load end. Plot all devices on the same log-log axes using software (ETAP, DAPPER, Easy Power, or DIgSILENT PowerFactory — all used by Pakistani engineering consultancies).
  5. Check grading margins: Verify 0.3–0.4 s margin at the maximum through-fault current at each grading point.
  6. Verify IEC 60947-2 selectivity: Check manufacturer tables for Type 1 or Type 2 selectivity at the declared fault current.
  7. Check thermal limits: Device let-through I²t must not exceed cable and equipment withstand I²t.
  8. Submit to utility: For 11 kV connections to LESCO/K-Electric, submit protection study with relay setting schedule for approval.

6. Zone Selective Interlocking (ZSI) — Achieving Instantaneous Discrimination

Traditional time-graded discrimination sacrifices fault clearance speed at upstream devices. ZSI addresses this by allowing the upstream ACB/MCCB to operate instantaneously when no downstream device has detected the fault (zone signal absent), while delaying for a controlled period when a downstream device has detected and is clearing a fault.

How ZSI works:

  1. Each device continuously monitors for overcurrent exceeding the instantaneous threshold (Ii).
  2. When a downstream device detects overcurrent, it sends a restraint signal (zone signal) to the upstream device.
  3. Upstream device receives zone signal: adds a short delay (typically 60–100 ms) — enough time for downstream to clear.
  4. If downstream clears the fault, upstream returns to monitoring. If downstream fails, the upstream operates after the delay.
  5. If upstream detects overcurrent with NO zone signal: operates instantaneously (typically <20 ms).

Pakistan application: SITE Karachi industrial complexes and Kot Lakhpat (Lahore) industrial estates with multiple tenants on a shared 11/0.415 kV substation use ZSI to prevent complete LT bus-bar blackout during a sub-feeder fault. Retrofit ZSI kits available from major MCCB brands for panels already installed without ZSI wiring — cost PKR 35,000–90,000 per panel.

8. NEPRA Grid Code 2023 — Protection Requirements for IPP and Consumer Connections

NEPRA's Grid Code (2023 revision) imposes specific requirements for protection systems at grid-connected generation and large consumer connections:

  • Fault clearance time: Primary protection must clear three-phase faults within 80–120 ms (4–6 cycles) at 11 kV and above. LV faults must clear within 100 ms for solar PV systems under NEM 3.0.
  • Back-up protection: Independent back-up relay required, separate CT circuit, separate DC supply.
  • Protection study submission: All connections >200 kVA at 11 kV must submit protection coordination study and relay setting schedule for NEPRA/utility approval before energisation.
  • Anti-islanding protection: Solar PV inverter systems must include under/overvoltage and under/overfrequency relay, loss-of-mains detection (ROCOF ≥ 1 Hz/s or vector shift ≥ 6°) per NEM 3.0 Technical Standard.
  • Earth fault detection: Neutral earthing resistor (NER) on 415 V LV bus recommended for industrial installations >1 MVA. NER limits earth fault current to 5–50 A, protecting equipment and enabling sensitive earth fault relay operation.
