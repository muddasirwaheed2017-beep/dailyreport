1. Why Capacity Testing is Critical in Pakistan

Pakistan's industrial UPS installations face a unique combination of stressors that accelerate battery degradation and make age-based replacement unreliable:

  • Loadshedding frequency: 4–14 hours per day of discharge-recharge cycling in industrial zones. IEC battery life models assume 1–2 discharge cycles per year; Pakistani installations see 200–600 cycles per year. This compresses a 5-year calendar life into 2–3 functional years.
  • Partial State of Charge (PSOC) cycling: Short loadshedding intervals (30–60 minutes) prevent full recharge before the next outage. VRLA batteries operating chronically at PSOC develop lead sulfate crystal growth that permanently reduces available capacity.
  • Temperature extremes: Battery rooms in Sindh and southern Punjab reach 45–50°C in summer without active cooling. Each 10°C above 25°C roughly halves VRLA calendar life. At 45°C, a 5-year design life becomes approximately 18 months.
  • Generator harmonic loading: When UPS rectifiers draw from diesel generators, the resulting harmonic currents (THD 18–35%) stress battery strings differently than clean grid charging.

The consequence: capacity testing every 12 months (per IEEE 1188) is the minimum justified by Pakistan conditions. High-criticality installations (hospitals, telecommunications, data centres) should test every 6 months.

3. IEC 60896-21/22 VRLA Capacity Test Procedure (Step-by-Step)

IEC 60896-21 (VRLA batteries for stationary applications, Part 21: Methods of test) defines the reference capacity test procedure. The following is the procedure as applied to industrial UPS battery strings in Pakistan.

3.1 Pre-Test Conditions (IEC 60896-21 Clause 5.4)

  1. Full charge: Apply a constant voltage charge at C20 rate until current drops to ≤2% of C20 for 3 consecutive hours. Do not begin capacity test within 24 hours of a discharge event.
  2. Temperature stabilisation: Allow battery string to stabilise at reference temperature (25°C ± 2°C) for a minimum of 4 hours. Record ambient temperature at start and end of test — temperature correction will be applied if outside ±2°C band.
  3. Record initial values: Open circuit voltage per cell, string float voltage, ambient temperature, battery age, last test date.

3.2 Discharge Procedure (IEC 60896-21 Clause 5.4.3)

  1. Connect calibrated discharge load bank (constant current, rated to ≥110% of C10 current).
  2. Set discharge current to I₁₀ = C10 / 10 (10-hour rate). For a 100 Ah battery: I₁₀ = 10 A.
  3. Record cell voltages every 30 minutes during discharge, and every 1 minute during the final 10% of expected discharge time.
  4. Stop discharge when any cell reaches 1.80 V/cell (IEC 60896-21 endpoint voltage for VRLA AGM at C10 rate, 25°C).
  5. Record total discharge time (T_actual).

3.3 Capacity Calculation

Actual capacity (Ah) = I₁₀ × T_actual (hours)

Capacity as percentage of rated: C% = (Actual Ah ÷ Rated C10 Ah) × 100

Pass: C% ≥ 80%. Fail: C% < 80% (IEC 60896-21 / IEEE 1188 replacement criterion).

Testing at elevated temperature masks capacity loss and leads to catastrophic failures during critical load events.

4. IEC 62619 & Lithium Battery Testing

LiFePO4 (LFP) batteries are increasingly used in Pakistani industrial UPS installations due to their superior cycle life (2,000+ cycles vs 300–500 for VRLA) and lower sensitivity to partial state of charge cycling. IEC 62619 (Safety requirements for secondary lithium cells and batteries for use in industrial applications) governs testing.

4.2 LiFePO4 BMS Data Extraction Procedure

Before performing a full discharge test on LFP batteries, extract BMS data via Modbus RTU or CAN bus:

  • Cycle count (replace if >2,000 cycles at 80% DoD)
  • State of Health (SoH) register — compare to rated capacity
  • Cell voltage balance — if spread >50mV at rest, perform cell balancing before capacity test
  • Maximum historical cell temperature — thermal abuse history not visible in capacity test
  • Minimum historical cell voltage — deep discharge events not recoverable in LFP chemistry

5. Discharge Methodologies: CC vs CR vs CP

Three discharge methodologies apply to different UPS and industrial load profiles. The selection affects both the accuracy of capacity assessment and the relevance to actual service conditions.

Battery Discharge Methodology Comparison
Method Description Load Bank Type Required Best For Pakistan Application
Constant Current (CC) Fixed current throughout discharge regardless of voltage drop Programmable CC load bank (e.g., 200A/50A banks) IEC 60896-21 compliance, VRLA testing NTDC substation DC system testing, industrial UPS acceptance testing
Constant Resistance (CR) Fixed resistance load — current decreases as battery voltage drops Resistive load banks (wire-wound or water-cooled) Telecom battery strings, simple field testing PTCL/telecom repeater hut battery checks
Constant Power (CP) Fixed power load — current increases as battery voltage drops Electronic load bank with CP mode UPS output simulation, data centre battery testing Data centres, hospitals — closest to real UPS load profile

Pakistan recommendation: Use Constant Current (CC) discharge for IEC 60896-21 compliance testing and NTDC acceptance tests. Use Constant Power (CP) for data centre and hospital UPS installations where the actual load profile is best represented by CP mode. The capacity result varies by up to 5% between methods, so always report the method used.

5.1 Discharge Rate Selection

The discharge rate significantly affects measured capacity. Standard rates for industrial UPS in Pakistan:

  • C10 rate (0.1C): Standard IEC 60896-21 rate. Conservative — gives highest measured capacity. Use for NTDC compliance tests.
  • C8 rate (0.125C): Common for telecommunications applications.
  • C3 rate (0.333C): Represents UPS applications where backup time is 30 minutes to 1 hour. Gives lower measured capacity — 5–10% below C10 for VRLA.
  • C1 rate (1C): For applications requiring 1-hour backup. Measured capacity 15–25% below C10 — important to specify when comparing battery quotes.

6. Impedance Spectroscopy & Internal Resistance Testing

Full discharge capacity testing requires 10–24 hours per battery string (discharge + recharge). Impedance testing provides a non-intrusive surrogate measurement that can be performed in 2–4 minutes per battery, enabling monthly screening without removing batteries from service.

6.2 Impedance Trending — The Right Approach

Internal resistance absolute values are not reliable SoH indicators in isolation — they vary significantly by battery design, age, and temperature. The correct approach is trending against baseline:

  1. Establish baseline impedance for each cell immediately after commissioning (or after a known-good capacity test).
  2. Test at the same time of year (to control temperature), same float voltage, same SoC.
  3. Alarm threshold: Any cell showing impedance increase >25% above its own baseline warrants investigation and capacity test scheduling.
  4. Replacement trigger: Any cell showing impedance increase >50% above baseline — replace that string regardless of capacity test result.

LiFePO4 limitation: Impedance testing is significantly less useful for LFP batteries because the internal resistance vs SoH curve is very flat (does not rise significantly until <70% SoH). For LFP, rely on BMS cycle count and occasional capacity test rather than impedance screening.

7. Temperature Derating & Pakistan Corrections

Pakistan's climate creates battery degradation patterns that differ fundamentally from IEC standard assumptions (25°C reference, 200 cycles/year). Engineers must apply the following Pakistan-specific corrections:

7.1 Arrhenius Life Derating for Pakistan Temperatures

VRLA battery calendar life at elevated temperature follows the Arrhenius equation. For each 10°C above 25°C, calendar life approximately halves:

  • 25°C: 100% of rated calendar life (manufacturer's standard)
  • 30°C: ~75% (Islamabad summer with AC)
  • 35°C: ~50% (unventilated room, spring/autumn)
  • 40°C: ~37% (common summer condition, Punjab industrial)
  • 45°C: ~25% (Sindh/Balochistan summer, no cooling)

A 5-year rated VRLA battery operated in a 40°C battery room effectively has a ~23-month functional life. Capacity testing at 18 months would detect the decline before a critical failure during loadshedding.

7.2 Seasonal Test Scheduling

Perform capacity tests before and after summer in Pakistan:

  • Pre-summer test (March–April): Establishes baseline before peak temperature stress. Identifies marginal batteries before the most demanding load season.
  • Post-summer test (October–November): Identifies batteries that degraded during summer. Allows winter battery procurement and replacement before next loadshedding season.

8. Seven Pakistan-Specific Battery Failure Modes

The following failure modes are disproportionately common in Pakistani industrial installations relative to IEC baseline assumptions:

  1. Loadshedding-Induced PSOC Sulfation: Short loadshedding intervals (2–4 hours) prevent full recharge. Chronic PSOC cycling forms stable lead sulfate crystals (hard sulfate) that permanently block plate surface area. Capacity degradation is progressive and non-recoverable without desulfation charging. Detection: Reduced C10 capacity with normal impedance — classic PSOC signature. Prevention: Programme UPS charger for extended equalization charging (14.4V/12V cell, 4–6 hours) weekly.
  2. Thermal Runaway from Charger Over-Voltage: Generator AVR instability (common with old Cummins and Perkins sets, 10–15% voltage variation) causes UPS charger output to spike, applying over-voltage to VRLA batteries. VRLA batteries are permanently damaged by over-charge at >2.35 V/cell (float). Detection: Battery case swelling, electrolyte dry-out, capacity <60%. Prevention: Install input voltage monitoring at UPS rectifier; set rectifier over-voltage cutout at 2.40 V/cell.
  3. Parallel String Imbalance from Generator Switchover: When generator connects to UPS with residual voltage difference >5V, current surge creates unequal loading across parallel battery strings. Over years, strings diverge in SoH. A single string failing creates load redistribution that accelerates failure of remaining strings. Detection: Per-string current monitoring during discharge — strings showing >15% current deviation require individual capacity testing.
  4. Harmonic Charger Current Plate Erosion: Thyristor-based UPS rectifiers in older installations (manufactured pre-2010) draw high harmonic currents from the battery during partial operation. THD >20% at battery terminals causes micro-cracking of positive plate active material, leading to capacity fade before impedance increase. Detection: Capacity test only — impedance test misses this failure mode. Corrective action: replace with IGBT rectifier or install line reactor.
  5. Capacity Fade from Repeated Depth-of-Discharge (DoD) Variation: In mixed loadshedding schedules (1-hour outages Monday-Thursday, 8-hour outages Friday-Sunday in some industrial zones), battery strings experience variable DoD cycles. Variable DoD accelerates plate shedding and reduces cycle life by 20–30% compared to consistent DoD cycling. Detection: Capacity trending — rapid decline after 18–24 months of service.
  6. Water Quality Sulfation in Flooded Lead-Acid: WAPDA older installations and rural DISCO substations still use flooded vented lead-acid batteries topped up with local water. High TDS (total dissolved solids) water — common in Karachi and central Punjab tube-well areas — introduces chlorides and sulfates that contaminate electrolyte, accelerating positive grid corrosion. Detection: Electrolyte SG measurement below 1.200 despite full charge, combined with poor capacity. Prevention: Use only deionised water (DI) for electrolyte topping; minimum 0.1 µS/cm conductivity.
  7. Temperature Gradient String Imbalance: Battery rooms with poor ventilation develop temperature gradients of 8–15°C between batteries on lower shelves (near floor exhaust vent) and upper shelves (near ceiling). Lower-temperature batteries deliver less capacity per cycle, cycling deeper and degrading faster. Over 2–3 years, the string becomes heterogeneous — individual batteries test at 95% while adjacent batteries test at 65%. Detection: Per-battery impedance scan identifies the gradient pattern. Prevention: Install directional fans to equalise temperature within ±3°C across the string.

9. NTDC FAT & WAPDA Acceptance Test Requirements

Pakistani utility (NTDC, WAPDA DISCOs) procurement specifications require Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) and Site Acceptance Testing (SAT) for all battery systems supplied for substations and grid infrastructure.

9.1 NTDC Battery FAT Requirements (PMM-P-2024 Reference)

  • Capacity test: 3-hour rate (C3) at 25°C ± 2°C. Minimum 100% rated capacity at FAT.
  • Internal resistance: Measured per cell at 25°C. Must match factory test certificate within ±5%.
  • Cell voltage balance: All cells within ±0.05V at end of charge.
  • Thermal imaging: Infrared scan of all cells and inter-cell connectors at C3 discharge. No cell to exceed ambient + 10°C.
  • Documentation: Cell formation report, factory test certificate, traceability to IEC 60896-21 or IEC 62619 test procedure.

9.2 WAPDA DISCO Substation Battery Acceptance (Operations Manual 2022)

  • DC system voltage: 110V DC (±5%) for 33kV+ substations; 48V DC for 11kV outdoor DISCOs.
  • Floating voltage: 2.23 V/cell (VRLA AGM) or per manufacturer recommendation.
  • Equalization charge: Mandatory quarterly at 2.30 V/cell for 4 hours for flooded cells; VRLA per manufacturer schedule.
  • Annual capacity test: 10-hour rate (C10), with temperature correction applied. Results logged in substation maintenance register.
  • Minimum acceptable capacity for continued service: 80% of rated. Below 80%: mandatory replacement within 90 days.

11. 10-Point Battery Capacity Test Checklist

  1. Pre-test full charge confirmed: Float current stabilised at ≤2% of C20 for 3 consecutive hours before test.
  2. Temperature recorded: Ambient temperature at start and end of test; temperature correction factor identified from IEC 60896-21 Annex A table.
  3. Battery age and last test date recorded: Critical context for trending analysis.
  4. Discharge method selected and documented: CC / CR / CP; discharge rate (C10 / C8 / C3 / C1); end-voltage threshold.
  5. Load bank calibration current: Verify load bank actual current against specification with calibrated clamp meter. Discrepancy >2%: recalibrate before test.
  6. Per-cell voltage monitoring connected: All cells individually monitored; data logger configured for 30-minute intervals with 1-minute intervals in final 10% of expected discharge.
  7. Parallel string isolation confirmed: Each string tested individually (unless string isolation impossible — document if testing aggregate).
  8. Safety PPE worn: Arc flash PPE per NFPA 70E (see Arc Flash Risk Assessment PPE Guide); acid-resistant gloves for VRLA/flooded cells; eye protection.
  9. Temperature correction applied: Corrected capacity calculated per IEC 60896-21 Annex A. Report both raw and corrected values.
  10. Test report prepared and signed: Includes battery make/model/serial, date, temperature, discharge method, raw and corrected capacity, pass/fail result, recommendation.

12. Seven Common Mistakes in Battery Capacity Testing (Pakistan)

  1. Testing at elevated temperature and omitting correction: The most common error in Pakistani facilities. A battery at 42°C delivering 83% capacity is actually at 75.5% corrected capacity — a failing battery. Apply IEC 60896-21 Annex A corrections always.
  2. Testing within 24 hours of a discharge event: Batteries require a full recharge stabilisation period. Testing immediately after loadshedding gives artificially low results that mislead replacement decisions.
  3. Testing the aggregate string rather than individual strings: When parallel strings are tested together, a failing string can be masked by a healthy string. Always isolate and test each string separately.
  4. Using impedance testing as the sole SoH method for VRLA: Impedance screens effectively but misses PSOC sulfation and harmonic plate erosion that do not manifest as increased impedance until late-stage failure. Combine impedance trending with annual capacity tests.
  5. Applying VRLA capacity pass/fail thresholds to LiFePO4: The 80% threshold applies to both, but LFP batteries may show 95% capacity with a BMS cycle count of 2,100 — indicating end of cycle life despite good current capacity. Always check BMS cycle count alongside capacity result for LFP.
  6. Ignoring generator frequency variation effects: When testing UPS batteries supplied from a generator, verify generator frequency is 50 Hz ± 0.5 Hz before test. Frequency hunting (common with AVR instability) affects UPS rectifier output and creates inconsistent charging that invalidates the pre-test full charge condition.
  7. No per-cell monitoring during discharge: Testing string voltage only misses individual weak cells that reach 1.80V cutoff early, masking true string capacity. A single reversed cell in a string can cause premature cutoff at apparently normal string voltage. Per-cell monitoring is mandatory for accurate IEC 60896-21 compliance.

13. FAQ — Battery Capacity Testing Pakistan

How often should I test battery capacity in Pakistan given loadshedding conditions?
For VRLA batteries in facilities with >6 hours/day average loadshedding: every 6 months. For VRLA with <6 hours/day: every 12 months per IEEE 1188. For LiFePO4: every 24 months plus BMS cycle count monitoring. After any discharge event >80% DoD, schedule a capacity test within 60 days.
What is the correct replacement threshold for VRLA batteries?
IEC 60896-21 and IEEE 1188 both specify 80% of rated capacity (C10, 25°C corrected) as the replacement threshold. In Pakistan, proactive replacement at 75% is recommended for critical loads (hospitals, data centres) to allow procurement lead time — imported VRLA batteries have 4–8 week delivery lead times. Local VRLA assembly is available for standard sizes but quality varies.
Can impedance testing replace full capacity testing?
No. Impedance testing is a screening tool to identify batteries that warrant full capacity testing. IEEE 1188 explicitly states that impedance testing does not replace capacity testing — it complements it. In Pakistan's PSOC environment, capacity degradation can precede impedance increase by 12–18 months. Annual capacity tests cannot be replaced by impedance screening alone.
What is the minimum equipment needed for field battery testing in Pakistan?
Minimum viable kit: programmable electronic load bank (CC mode, ≥200A), per-cell voltage logger (4-channel minimum, 16-channel preferred for 48V strings), calibrated DC clamp meter, temperature probe with datalogger, and a laptop for test report generation. Total investment: PKR 380,000–650,000 for a professional field testing kit. Alternatively, contract testing services are available at PKR 28,000–85,000 per string.
How do I comply with NTDC requirements for battery capacity testing?
NTDC PMM-P-2024 requires: (a) C3 rate capacity test at FAT with 100% minimum result; (b) annual C10 capacity tests in service with 80% minimum threshold; (c) all results documented in the substation battery register; (d) IEC 60896-21 or IEC 62619 procedure compliance. The test report must include test temperature, correction factor applied, individual cell voltages at end of discharge, and pass/fail conclusion signed by a licensed electrical engineer.
Is there a difference between testing VRLA batteries in 110V DC substation systems vs 48V UPS systems?
The procedure is identical (IEC 60896-21), but the load bank must be rated for the higher voltage (110V DC strings have 55 × 2V cells in series, with end-of-discharge at 55 × 1.80V = 99V). Most industrial load banks cover 0–125V DC; verify rating before test. The string capacity (Ah) calculation is the same. Per-cell monitoring is more critical in longer strings as individual weak cells cause premature string cutoff at apparently normal aggregate voltages.
