Why Thermography Matters More in Pakistan Than Almost Anywhere Else

Three Pakistani realities amplify the value of thermal inspection beyond international norms:

  1. WAPDA voltage sags and restart stress. NTDC's 220 kV grid experiences an average of 4.2 voltage dip events per day during summer peak (May–August). Each sag causes transient current surges through loose connections. A joint running at ΔT = 15°C under normal load can spike to ΔT = 60°C during restart — well into the NFPA 70B "Priority 1 — repair immediately" threshold (ΔT > 40°C vs ambient on electrical equipment). Without thermal scanning, this damage accumulates silently.
  2. NEECA SRO 1065(I)/2023 energy audits. The National Energy Efficiency and Conservation Authority now requires Large Energy Users (LEUs) — factories consuming >500 MWh/year — to submit triennial energy audits. Thermographic surveys of transformer banks, motor circuits, and compressed-air leaks are explicitly listed as accepted audit methodologies under the SRO annex. Factories with a documented thermography programme reduce their audit compliance cost by 20–35% because the thermal data satisfies multiple SRO line items simultaneously.
  3. High ambient temperatures compress safety margins. When ambient is 48°C (Multan, June), a motor that runs 70°C above ambient (ΔT = 70°C) is running at 118°C surface temperature — above the Class F winding limit (155°C total) by only 37°C. The same motor in Stockholm runs at 90°C total — a 65°C safety margin. Pakistan's thermal budget is roughly half of European equivalents. Thermography reveals which motors are approaching absolute limits rather than relative limits.

NFPA 70B Electrical Thermography — Application in Pakistani Panels

NFPA 70B (2023, Recommended Practice for Electrical Equipment Maintenance) is the global reference standard for electrical thermographic surveys. While not legally mandatory in Pakistan, it is increasingly required by:

  • ISO 50001 / NEECA energy management auditors
  • Export-market buyers (EU textile sourcing protocols, CTPAT for US-bound shipments)
  • Insurance underwriters for industrial property coverage
  • NTDC Grid Code annex requirements for industrial consumers above 1 MW

Pakistan-specific calibration note: NFPA 70B uses ΔT above a reference temperature (a similar phase or a known-good connection). Do NOT compare directly to ambient — a 48°C ambient will cause most panels to read elevated even when healthy. Always use the same-phase same-load reference methodology in Pakistani summer conditions.

Electrical vs Mechanical Thermography — Scope and Techniques

Electrical Thermography Targets

  • Main distribution boards (MDB) and sub-distribution boards (SDB) — busbar connections, ACB/MCCB terminals, earth bars
  • Motor control centres (MCC) — contactor tips, overload relay elements, cable glands
  • Power transformers (11/0.415 kV) — HV bushings, tap changer, cooling fins, oil fill point (correlates with dissolved gas analysis findings)
  • VFD panels — DC bus capacitor banks, IGBT heatsinks, input reactor connections (see VFD buyer guide for thermal derating guidelines)
  • Solar PV string combiner boxes — bypass diode hot spots, MC4 connectors, fuse holders (IEC 62446-3 inspection)
  • UPS inverter sections — battery terminal connections, inverter IGBT modules

Mechanical Thermography Targets

  • Electric motor bearings — compare DE (drive end) vs NDE (non-drive end) symmetry; asymmetric ΔT > 5°C indicates bearing degradation. Cross-reference with vibration monitoring for dual confirmation
  • Pump and gearbox seals — lip seal friction, mechanical seal face temperature
  • Steam traps — failed-open traps show as hot; failed-closed traps show as cold — thermography finds both failure modes with one pass
  • Refractory and insulation integrity — furnace walls, kiln shells, boiler casing hot spots
  • Compressed air piping leaks — not directly visible, but thermography finds insulation gaps that indicate leak locations

Step-by-Step Thermographic Survey Procedure (IEC/ISO Compliant)

  1. Pre-survey preparation. Confirm equipment is under ≥40% rated load (NFPA 70B minimum load requirement for valid electrical survey). Record ambient temperature, humidity, and time. Obtain a one-line diagram of the electrical installation. For panel inspections, confirm Safe System of Work (SSoW) and arc-flash boundary — a 320°C IR lens is not PPE arc-flash protection.
  2. Equipment setup. Set camera emissivity: electrical insulation and painted metal ε = 0.90–0.95; bare copper busbars ε = 0.03–0.07 (use electrical tape reference patch); transformer oil ε ≈ 0.95; motor casing (painted) ε = 0.90. Set camera to isothermal (colour palette) mode with span matched to expected ΔT range (typically ±15°C from reference for electrical surveys).
  3. Systematic scan sequence. Follow a fixed sequence documented in the thermography procedure: MDB → SDB → MCC → transformer → motor fleet. Use a grid numbering system matching the one-line diagram. Each panel door must be open for a minimum 5-minute thermal soak after opening before recording. Closed-panel thermography through inspection windows is acceptable for 11 kV equipment but requires camera NETD ≤30 mK and known emissivity of window material.
  4. Data capture and annotation. Record each thermal image with: timestamp, equipment ID, load at time of scan (amps from clamp meter), ambient temperature, emissivity setting, minimum/maximum/average temperature of ROI (Region of Interest). Save paired visible-light + thermal images. Modern cameras with MSX (multi-spectral dynamic imaging) blend both automatically.
  5. Report generation (ISO 18436-7 Level II minimum). Issue findings table sorted by Priority (1–4). Include NFPA 70B ΔT classification, recommended action, and next-inspection date. Attach to plant data historian or CMMS (Computerised Maintenance Management System) as a time-stamped asset record. For NEECA SRO 1065 audit files, the Level II thermographer's ISO 18436-7 certificate number must appear on the report header.
  6. Post-repair verification. Always re-scan repaired connections under the same load conditions within 48 hours of repair. Document the before/after thermal pair. This verification image is the most valuable single document in the thermography programme — it proves the repair worked and closes the NFPA 70B work order.

Integrating Thermography with Other Predictive Maintenance Technologies

Infrared thermography is most powerful as one layer in a combined condition monitoring stack:

  • Thermography + Vibration: A motor with elevated bearing temperature AND elevated vibration (ISO 20816 Zone C/D) is a confirmed failure candidate — plan replacement within 1 week. Either indicator alone may trigger monitoring; both together trigger intervention. See the vibration monitoring buyer guide for sensor selection.
  • Thermography + Energy monitoring: A motor running 15°C hotter than its twin on the same process line almost always draws 3–8% more current. Cross-reference with the EMS sub-metering data to quantify the energy waste and calculate the repair ROI before spending on the work order.
  • Thermography + IIoT: Fixed-mount thermal cameras feeding alert data to an IIoT gateway via MQTT close the gap between quarterly walk-around surveys — especially valuable for critical transformers, MDB main incomer, and kiln drive panels where a thermal event between survey dates is unacceptable. The gateway pushes a WhatsApp/SMS alert within 90 seconds of a threshold breach at any hour, including 02:00 on a Tuesday during load shedding.
  • Motor protection relay + thermography: A digital motor protection relay with thermal memory will detect running overtemperature but only acts at the winding thermal model level. Thermography finds the external contributors — a clogged cooling fin, a blocked duct, an overloaded cable terminal — that the relay cannot see.

Top 5 Thermography Mistakes in Pakistani Industrial Facilities

  1. Scanning panels under light load. NFPA 70B requires ≥40% load. Surveying during Ramadan low-shift (30% load) will miss Priority 2–3 findings that only manifest under full load. Always coordinate thermography with production peak shifts. In textile mills, this means 08:00–16:00 on a weekday.
  2. Incorrect emissivity settings on bare copper. Bare copper busbars have emissivity of 0.03–0.07 depending on oxidation state. Setting emissivity to 0.95 (the camera default) will read a 100°C busbar as approximately 42°C — a 58°C underread that turns a Priority 1 finding invisible. Always apply a strip of black electrical tape as a reference patch on critical busbar connections before scanning.
  3. Not compensating for ambient temperature in summer. At 48°C ambient, the camera will flag many components as "warm" that are entirely normal. Use ΔT methodology (compare to a reference of the same type under the same load) rather than absolute temperature thresholds. A well-connected MCB terminal at 55°C absolute (ΔT = +7°C vs ambient) is Priority 4 / Monitor — do not trigger emergency shutdowns based on absolute temperature alone.
  4. Skipping post-repair verification images. Without a post-repair thermal, there is no documented proof the repair was effective. A contactor tip that looked clean and had a loose screw behind it will read hot again after one month if only the tip was cleaned and the screw retorqued at 2 Nm instead of the specified 5 Nm. The re-scan catches this; the initial repair report alone does not.
  5. Using Level I-only thermographers for NEECA SRO audit documentation. The SRO annex specifies "qualified thermographer" with reference to ISO 18436-7. NEECA auditors increasingly ask for the certification number. A Level I thermographer performing surveys is compliant only if they are under the supervision of a Level II/III who signs the report. Sending a Level I-only report to NEECA risks audit non-conformance and an SRO penalty notice.

Building a Thermography Programme: Checklist for Pakistani Factories

  • ☐ Define criticality classes (Class 1 = general, Class 3 = critical production/safety) for all electrical equipment per NFPA 70B Annex A
  • ☐ Select camera class (minimum 320×240, NETD ≤60 mK for Class 3 panels)
  • ☐ Certify at least one in-house thermographer to ISO 18436-7 Level II
  • ☐ Write equipment-specific survey procedures (emissivity settings, load requirements, inspection distance, PPE requirements per arc-flash boundary)
  • ☐ Create equipment asset register with thermal baseline images at known-good state under full load
  • ☐ Set inspection frequencies: Class 3 = quarterly, Class 1 = annual (minimum; more frequent in high-humidity locations — Karachi port-area facilities often double frequencies)
  • ☐ Integrate thermal reports into CMMS (or at minimum, a structured folder system linked to the asset register)
  • ☐ Archive NEECA SRO-required documentation: thermographer certificate, Level II-signed report, equipment ID cross-reference to NEECA asset list
  • ☐ Install fixed-mount cameras on Priority 1 assets (main transformer, MDB main incomer, kiln drive panel) if budget allows — connect to IIoT gateway for 24/7 monitoring

Frequently Asked Questions — Infrared Thermography Pakistan

Q: What is the minimum load required before performing an electrical thermographic survey in Pakistan?
NFPA 70B (2023) requires a minimum of 40% of rated load. In Pakistani factories, coordinate thermography with peak production shifts to ensure adequate load. Surveys conducted during low-load periods (e.g. Ramadan reduced shifts, early morning) will miss temperature rise on Priority 2–3 findings and produce a false sense of electrical health.
Q: Which ISO standard governs thermographer certification in Pakistan?
ISO 18436-7:2014 defines three competency levels (Level I, II, III). NEECA SRO 1065(I)/2023 audit documentation requires a Level II-certified thermographer to sign thermography reports for Large Energy User compliance purposes.
Q: What thermal imaging camera resolution is recommended for Pakistani industrial panels?
Minimum 320×240 pixels, NETD ≤60 mK for electrical panel surveys. For 11 kV/33 kV substation equipment, 640×480 with NETD ≤30 mK. Entry-level 160×120 cameras are adequate for initial walk-arounds but not for ISO 18436-7 Level II diagnostic work.
Q: What emissivity setting should I use for bare copper busbars?
Bare copper has very low emissivity (0.03–0.07). The camera default of 0.95 will underread by 50–60°C. Apply black electrical tape as a reference patch and use that temperature as the measurement reference for all adjacent bare metal.
Q: How does WAPDA load shedding affect thermographic inspection planning?
Plan surveys within 1–2 hours after plant restart from a WAPDA outage — loose connections reach their highest ΔT during restart transients and are most visible. The June–August peak demand window (maximum NTDC voltage dip frequency) also increases finding rate.
Q: Can thermography replace transformer oil testing (DGA)?
No — they are complementary. Thermography detects surface hotspots; DGA detects internal insulation faults that thermography cannot see through the tank wall. Use thermography quarterly + DGA annually for complete transformer health coverage.
Q: What is the NFPA 70B Priority 1 temperature rise threshold?
ΔT > 40°C above a reference connection under the same load. In 48°C Pakistani summer ambient, an absolute busbar joint temperature of 88°C+ is a Priority 1 finding requiring immediate de-energisation.
Q: What thermal camera price should I budget for a Pakistani industrial thermography programme?
Professional programme (320×240 or 640×480 camera, ISO 18436-7 Level II capable): PKR 1,200,000–1,800,000 including import duties. Entry-level (160×120): PKR 380,000–550,000. All Q4 2026 estimates including Pakistan Customs HS 9025.1990 duties.
