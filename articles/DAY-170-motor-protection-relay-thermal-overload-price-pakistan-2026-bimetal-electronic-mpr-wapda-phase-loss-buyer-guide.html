1. Types of Motor Protection: Bimetal TOL vs Electronic EOL vs Full MPR

Motor protection devices fall into three tiers, each with distinct capabilities and price points relevant to Pakistan's industrial and agricultural sectors.

1.1 Bimetal Thermal Overload Relay (TOL)

The bimetal TOL uses two bonded metal strips that bend with heat when excess current flows through the heater element. When the bimetallic strip deflects past the trip point, it opens the normally-closed (NC) contact, dropping the contactor coil and isolating the motor. Key Pakistani application notes:

  • Ambient temperature derating: Standard TOLs are calibrated at 20 °C. At the 45–50 °C ambient common in Pakistani factory floors June–September, a TOL rated for 10A may trip at 8–8.5A without manual compensation. Adjustable bimetal TOLs allow ambient compensation via a rotary dial — specify this feature for Karachi and inland sites.
  • Phase-loss sensitivity: Basic bimetal TOLs without differential protection may take 3–8 minutes to trip on single-phasing of a lightly-loaded motor — causing severe winding damage. Phase-loss sensitive designs (differential bimetal construction) trip in <30 seconds. Always specify phase-loss sensitive TOLs for 3-phase motors above 2.2kW.
  • Reset: Manual reset (MR) preferred in Pakistan for safety interlock compliance; auto-reset (AR) only where unmanned restart is designed-in with a risk assessment.
  • IEC current classes: 0.1A to 70A in standard frame sizes; above 70A use electronic or bus-bar type relays.

1.2 Electronic Overload Relay (EOL)

Electronic overload relays use current transformers (CTs) and a microprocessor thermal model to calculate motor temperature rise. Advantages over bimetal in Pakistani conditions:

  • Accurate thermal memory: The electronic thermal model remembers previous thermal state across repeated start cycles — critical for pumps started multiple times daily on WAPDA supply.
  • Phase imbalance detection: Trips within 1–3 seconds on phase imbalance > 10–15% (adjustable) — far faster than bimetal.
  • Thermistor input: PTC/NTC thermistor input (most models from 7.5kW upward) allows direct winding temperature monitoring — essential for motors in poorly ventilated enclosures or dusty environments.
  • Earth fault detection: Integrated residual current sensing (some models) trips on ground fault <300mA — important for WAPDA earthing quality issues.
  • Trip logging and diagnostics: LED or display shows last-trip cause (OL / PL / PH imbalance / thermistor) — reduces troubleshooting time for site electricians.

1.3 Full-Function Motor Protection Relay (MPR)

Full MPRs (also called multi-function motor protection relays or motor management relays) incorporate comprehensive protection functions in one DIN-rail or panel-mount unit:

  • Thermal overload (adjustable class)
  • Phase loss / phase sequence
  • Phase voltage imbalance (adjustable threshold)
  • Overvoltage / undervoltage (critical for WAPDA 11kV feeders that sag to 360V or spike to 440V)
  • Stall / locked rotor (high-current extended duration)
  • Underload / dry-run (undercurrent trip — essential for Pakistani water pumps prone to dry running)
  • Earth fault
  • PTC/NTC thermistor input
  • Ground fault (sensitive earth fault)
  • Modbus RTU / RS-485 communication for SCADA integration

Full MPRs are justified for motors >15kW, critical process motors (boiler feed pumps, air compressors, crusher drives), and any motor where unplanned downtime costs more than the PKR 15,000–90,000 relay price.

2. Pakistan WAPDA Supply Threats to Motors — Why Standard Protection Fails

Pakistan's WAPDA and LESCO/IESCO/FESCO distribution grids exhibit supply characteristics that invalidate the European or Chinese factory defaults on most imported motor protection devices. Understanding these threats is essential for correct trip threshold configuration.

2.1 Phase Loss (Single Phasing)

Phase loss is the leading cause of motor burnout in Pakistan. When one phase of a 3-phase supply disappears — due to a blown fuse, loose connection, or feeder fault — the motor continues running on two phases. The remaining two phases carry √3 times the normal current, overheating the windings in minutes. WAPDA 11kV feeders in industrial estates experience 2–5 phase-loss events per month on average, peaking in summer when transformer loading and conductor sag cause fuse-link blowing.

Protection recommendation: Phase-loss sensitive bimetal TOL (Class 10 or 10A type) for motors <5.5kW; dedicated phase-loss relay or electronic EOL with <3-second trip for motors >5.5kW; full MPR with <0.5-second phase-loss trip for critical motors.

2.2 Voltage Imbalance

NEPRA's voltage imbalance limit is ±2% of nominal. In practice, Pakistani LV feeders commonly exhibit 3–8% imbalance from unequal single-phase loading. At 5% voltage imbalance, the negative-sequence current in the motor can reach 25% of rated current, heating the rotor at 6× the rated loss rate. Electronic EOLs should be set to trip at ≥10% imbalance (some installers set 15% to avoid nuisance trips — this is too high for 230V-class motors).

2.3 Voltage Sag and Dip

WAPDA nominal LV is 400V ±6% (IEC/PSQCA standard). In practice, rural and peri-urban feeders sag to 340–360V during peak hours. At 360V (10% under-voltage), motor current rises by ~12%, thermally loading a Class F motor beyond its safe continuous operating point. Motors running on DOL without an undervoltage relay will stall and draw locked-rotor current if voltage sags below ~75% of rated — this is a stall protection function only full MPRs provide.

2.4 Restart After Power Cut

Load-shedding-induced power restoration events cause simultaneous motor starts across a feeder, causing severe voltage dips and motor stress. Configure anti-restart delay (also called start inhibit or restart lockout) on EOL/MPR at minimum 60–120 seconds for Pakistan operations, and 300 seconds for compressors and large pumps with slow-closing check valves.

2.5 High Ambient Temperature

Standard IEC motors are rated at 40 °C ambient. Pakistani industrial environments regularly reach 45–50 °C in summer. For every 10 °C above rated ambient, motor life is halved (Arrhenius law). Configure the TOL/EOL current setting at 90–95% of motor FLA when ambient exceeds 45 °C, or use a thermistor-input EOL with actual winding temperature monitoring.

6. Wiring Principles: DOL with Thermal Overload Relay

A correctly wired DOL motor starter with thermal overload relay follows a standard control circuit that Pakistani electricians should understand at three levels: power circuit, control circuit, and indication circuit.

6.1 Power Circuit

The three-phase power circuit runs: Incomer MCB / MCCB → Contactor (main contacts) → Thermal Overload Relay (through-current path) → Motor terminal box. The TOL passes all three phases of motor current through its heater elements without interrupting them directly — the TOL trip mechanism operates the auxiliary NC contact in the control circuit, which de-energizes the contactor coil and opens the main contacts.

See the MCCB buyer guide for correct upstream short-circuit protection sizing. The MCCB/fuse provides short-circuit protection; the TOL/EOL provides thermal overload protection — these are distinct functions and both are required.

6.2 Control Circuit

Standard Pakistani industrial DOL control circuit (230V AC control, single-phase derived from incomer):

  • L (phase) → Fuse F2 (2A) → E-Stop NC (2-channel for SIL applications — see safety relay guide) → TOL NC contact (95-96) → Start/Stop circuit → Contactor coil (A1-A2) → N (neutral)
  • Start button (NO) in parallel with contactor auxiliary NO (latching/self-hold contact)
  • Stop button (NC) in series
  • TOL NC contact (95-96) in series — opens on trip, removes latch and coil power

6.3 Indication Circuit

  • Running indicator: Contactor auxiliary NO → Green lamp
  • Trip indicator: TOL auxiliary NC (97-98 on most TOLs) → Red lamp + horn/buzzer relay
  • Trip alarm: Wire TOL 97-98 to DCS/SCADA input or remote alarm panel

6.4 Reset Configuration

Set to Manual Reset for all motors in Pakistan. Auto-reset is prohibited under Pakistani industrial safety practice (PSQCA PS:4756-2024 for safety) unless a risk assessment explicitly permits it. Manual reset requires a site electrician to attend the tripped motor, inspect for the cause, and clear the fault before restart — this is the correct industrial safety standard.

8. WAPDA Phase-Loss Case Study — 15kW Pump Motor, Lahore Industrial Estate 2025

A Lahore industrial estate suffered the burnout of three 15kW booster pump motors in one month. Post-failure analysis revealed all three burned due to single-phase operation from repeated LESCO feeder phase-loss events. The original DOL starters used basic bimetal TOLs without differential phase-loss sensitivity, with trip times measured at 6–8 minutes on single-phasing at 60% load. The motor insulation had reached Class B thermal limit (130 °C) in under 4 minutes during each event.

Solution applied: Electronic EOL with <2-second phase-loss trip, Class 10 trip class, thermistor input wired to motor PTC, 90-second restart inhibit. Set phase imbalance threshold to 10%. Total parts cost per motor protection upgrade: PKR 11,500 (electronic EOL + wiring).

Result (6-month follow-up): Five LESCO phase-loss events recorded on monitoring system. All five resulted in motor trips within 1.8 seconds. No motor burnouts. The PKR 11,500 per motor upgrade protected three 15kW motors each worth PKR 85,000–95,000 (replacement cost including rewind). Total protection investment: PKR 34,500. Protected asset value: PKR 270,000. ROI: 683% in first 6 months.

9. 20-Point Commissioning Checklist — Motor Protection Relay (Pakistan Field)

  1. Nameplate FLA confirmed: Motor nameplate full-load amps verified at site (not assumed from motor kW). Note if motor is rewound — rewind FLA may differ from original nameplate.
  2. TOL/EOL current range matched: Selected TOL/EOL current range covers motor FLA with adjustment range at approximately 90% of range maximum (avoid running at the top of the range).
  3. Ambient temperature noted: Panel/switchgear ambient temperature measured. If >45 °C, current setting reduced to 90% of FLA.
  4. Phase-loss sensitive model confirmed: TOL product specification sheet reviewed — confirm "phase-loss sensitive" or "differential bimetal" designation.
  5. Trip class set correctly: Class 10 for standard applications. Class 20 documented and justified for high-inertia loads.
  6. Phase imbalance threshold set: Electronic EOL/MPR phase imbalance threshold set to 10%. Confirm setting via relay parameter menu.
  7. Phase sequence protection enabled: Phase sequence protection enabled for all pump, fan, and compressor applications.
  8. Undervoltage/overvoltage thresholds set: (MPR only) 75% UV, 110% OV, delays per Table 6. Test by brief voltage simulation or use relay's self-test function.
  9. Restart inhibit delay set: Anti-restart delay configured per application type (60s general / 300s compressor). Verify by timing a manual trip-and-reset cycle.
  10. Reset mode confirmed Manual: Confirm TOL/EOL/MPR is in MANUAL RESET mode. Apply a test trip and verify relay does not auto-restart.
  11. Control circuit NC contact wired correctly: TOL/EOL NC contact (95-96) confirmed in series with contactor coil circuit. Apply test trip; confirm contactor drops out immediately.
  12. Indication circuit functional: Trip indicator lamp (red) confirms illumination on TOL trip. Trip alarm output wired and confirmed active.
  13. Thermistor input wired (if fitted): PTC thermistor wires from motor terminal box connected to EOL/MPR input. Confirm thermistor resistance at cold motor ≈ 100–250 Ω; alarm value approaches 3kΩ; trip at 3.4kΩ.
  14. Underload threshold set (pump/compressor): Underload trip set to 30–50% of FLA with 20-second delay. Verify underload trip setting is active.
  15. Earth fault detection checked (if fitted): Earth fault threshold set to 300mA for standard; 100mA for critical or wet-area motors.
  16. Modbus address assigned (MPR with comms): Modbus RTU address set, baud rate 9600 or 19200 confirmed. SCADA / HMI polling verified with live data display.
  17. Load test run completed: Motor run at full load for 15 minutes. Running current measured with clamp meter on all three phases. Confirm balance within ±5% of set FLA.
  18. Trip log cleared / initialized: Relay trip log cleared after commissioning to establish a clean baseline for future diagnostics.
  19. Documentation completed: Panel label updated with TOL/EOL model, serial, current setting, trip class. Photo of relay settings taken for maintenance records.
  20. Operator training: On-site operator instructed on: what a trip means, how to safely reset after investigating cause, who to call. Never reset without identifying the trip cause.

10. Frequently Asked Questions — Motor Protection in Pakistan

Q: Can I use one thermal overload relay to protect multiple motors?

No. Each motor requires its own overload relay, sized specifically to that motor's FLA. Sharing an overload relay across motors defeats the thermal protection purpose and is prohibited under IEC 60947-4-1 and PSQCA guidelines.

Q: My thermal overload relay keeps tripping in summer. What should I do?

First verify the motor is not actually overloaded (measure running current with a clamp meter). If current is below nameplate FLA: (1) reduce the TOL current setting to 90% of measured running current, (2) improve panel ventilation, or (3) upgrade to an electronic EOL with automatic ambient compensation. Do not simply raise the trip class — this masks a real problem.

Q: What happens to a thermal overload relay if installed on a soft starter or VFD output?

A bimetal TOL installed between a soft starter or VFD output and the motor will not operate correctly because the current waveform from the soft starter (phase-angle controlled) or VFD (PWM) contains harmonic content that the thermal bimetal cannot model accurately. For soft starter applications, use the soft starter's built-in electronic protection function or add an electronic EOL. For VFD applications, rely on the VFD's internal motor protection and add a PTC thermistor input to the VFD — never place a TOL on the VFD output.

Q: What is the difference between a thermal overload relay and a motor protection relay?

A thermal overload relay (TOL) protects only against overload (sustained overcurrent) and in phase-loss sensitive designs, against single-phasing. A motor protection relay (MPR) is a full-function device that additionally protects against voltage imbalance, overvoltage, undervoltage, stall, earth fault, dry-run (underload), thermistor trip, and phase sequence error. MPRs typically include Modbus communication for SCADA integration. The MPR is the recommended choice for all motors above 15kW or in critical applications.

Q: How often should thermal overload relays be tested and replaced in Pakistan?

Test annually by applying a controlled overload test or using the relay's built-in test button. Replace bimetal TOLs after 10–12 years of service or following any trip caused by a severe fault (locked rotor, sustained phase loss), as severe thermal cycling degrades the bimetal calibration. Electronic EOLs require less physical degradation concern but should have firmware reviewed every 5 years if manufacturer provides updates.
